Damping simulates energy loss (friction, air resistance, internal heat). Low Damping (e.g., 0% on the right): The cloth is extremely "bouncy." When dropped, it will oscillate back and forth for a very long time before coming to rest. It may even appear jittery or chaotic as energy is not being dissipated from the system. High Damping (e.g., 100% on the left): The motion appears "thick," as if the cloth is moving through honey. When dropped, it falls very slowly and settles into its resting position almost immediately without any swinging or bouncing.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

How did we implement the handling of collisions with spheres and planes:

Sphere Collisions

If a point mass is inside the sphere (distance to origin < radius), it gets bumped to the surface by projecting the direction vector from the origin to the point mass onto the sphere surface (normalize × radius). The new position is last_position + (1 - friction) × (tangent_point - last_position).

Plane Collisions

If a point mass crosses the plane (signed distances on opposite sides), the tangent point is found by linearly interpolating between last and current positions at the crossing. A small SURFACE_OFFSET keeps it just above the surface. The new position is last_position + (1 - friction) × (tangent_point + offset - last_position).

Integration in simulate

After Verlet integration and Provot constraint correction, every point mass is tested against every collision object via co->collide(pm), which adjusts its position in place if needed.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

How did we implement handling self-collisions:

Self-collisions are handled using spatial hashing for efficiency. At each time step, build_spatial_map partitions 3D space into boxes of dimensions w × h × t and maps each point mass to its corresponding box via hash_position. The hash is computed by truncating each coordinate to its box index and combining them using large primes via XOR to produce a unique float key.

In self_collide, each point mass looks up its spatial hash bucket and checks all candidate point masses in the same volume. If two point masses (excluding itself) are within 2 * thickness distance, a correction vector is computed to push them apart to exactly that distance. The final correction applied to the point mass is the average of all pairwise corrections, scaled down by simulation_steps for stability.

Part 5: Shaders

What is a Shader Program:

A shader program runs on the GPU in parallel for every vertex and fragment. The vertex shader transforms geometry (positions, normals) from model space to screen space and passes interpolated values to the fragment shader via varyings. The fragment shader then uses those interpolated values to compute the final pixel color, implementing lighting and material effects.

Blinn-Phong Shading Model:

Blinn-Phong combines three lighting components: ambient (constant base light regardless of geometry), diffuse (light scattered equally in all directions, dependent on the angle between the surface normal and light direction), and specular (mirror-like highlights dependent on the halfway vector between the light and view directions). Together they approximate how real surfaces interact with light.

Bump Mapping:

Bump mapping is a shading technique used to create the illusion of depth and texture on a surface without actually modifying the underlying geometry. It works by altering the surface normals (the vectors perpendicular to the surface that dictate how light bounces off it) on a per-fragment (pixel) basis using a height map (texture).

The Result: When light hits the surface, the modified normals trick the lighting equation into creating shadows and highlights as if the surface were bumpy. However, if you look at the silhouette (the edge) of the object, it will still be perfectly smooth because the actual 3D mesh hasn't changed.

Displacement Mapping:

Displacement mapping, on the other hand, physically alters the geometry of the object. It uses the exact same height map texture, but instead of just faking the normals, it physically moves (displaces) the actual vertices of the mesh along their normal vectors based on the texture's values. It also updates the normals to match the new geometry.

The Result: The surface actually becomes bumpy. The silhouette of the object will appear jagged and uneven, and the displaced geometry can even cast real shadows on itself.

Varying coarseness:

-o 16 -a 16:

-o 128 -a 128:

It is clear from the displacement shading screenshots how the sphere's mesh resolution (coarseness) impacts the render. Because displacement mapping physically alters the height of the vertices, a higher resolution mesh (128 for longitude and latitude) allows the displaced surface details to become much more pronounced and accurate. On the other hand, it is difficult to tell any difference between the two resolution settings when using bump shading. This is because bump shading only manipulates the surface normals based on the texture—mapping the illusion of depth onto the sphere—without actually changing the sphere's physical surface geometry at all. Thus, the underlying mesh coarseness does not affect bump shading nearly as much as it affects displacement shading.
